# G1. Running the Game

> _"Know, O prince, that between the years when the oceans drank Atlantis and the gleaming cities, and the years of the rise of the sons of Aryas, there was an age undreamed of."_
> _- Robert E. Howard_

This chapter is short on purpose. The genre's full definition is in **P1 §1** and the canon is in **PA3 Inspirational Influences**. Other books teach generic GM craft better than this one could. What follows is the distillation - the few things specific to running a Warrior \* Sorcerer \* Rogue game. Adjudication advice (theatre-of-mind combat, group stealth, chases, mass combat) lives in **G6 Hazards and Spot Rules**.

---

## 1. The Style

Pulp action-adventure in an ancient world. The PCs chase gold and glory through ruins, cities, and wilderness that were old when their grandfathers were young. Other humans are usually the adversary - rival mercenaries, corrupt officials, scheming sorcerers, the priests of an inconvenient cult. Beasts and the restless dead are real, but the knife in the dark is more common than the dragon. Magic is dangerous and always costs the caster something. Ancient gods gnaw at the borders of reality; the world is older than its inhabitants admit, and what slumbers beneath the foundations did not sleep willingly.

A few things to keep out of the game:

- **No chosen ones.** The PCs are competent people who chose this life. They are not destined.
- **No Dark Lords.** Villains in this genre are small, specific, and motivated: a sorcerer after one particular relic; a slaver running one particular ring. Sauron does not belong here. Thoth-Amon does.
- **No saving the village for free.** PCs act for coin, connection, or personal stake. Heroism without payment is the wrong genre.
- **No magic as toolkit.** Every spell costs something. A sorcerer who casts freely without consequence has stopped being a sorcerer.
- **No clean victories.** Successes have a price; that price is what makes the story sword & sorcery instead of high fantasy.

---

## 2. The Sources

You cannot run a tone you do not know. Before your first session, read at least one story each from Howard, Leiber, and Moorcock. PA3 names specific stories worth starting with and lists the broader canon. If a sorcerer PC is at the table, read one Clark Ashton Smith story as well - his sorcerers are the template for what a sorcerer-villain should feel like.

When a session feels wrong, return to the source material. The fog clears.
