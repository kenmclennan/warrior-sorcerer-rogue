# G2. NPCs and Adversaries

> _"There were at least a score of them - hard, lean, wolfish men, with a sprinkling of black-bearded nomads among them."_
> _- Robert E. Howard_

This chapter gives you the tools to build every human enemy the PCs will face: the faceless rabble who can be scattered with a shout, the seasoned professionals who earn their pay, and the named antagonists who will make the players hate them and dread them in equal measure. It covers stat block formats, design principles for each tier, and five ready-to-use examples drawn from WSR's ancient-world frame.

The player-facing view of the mook rule - what the players see at the table - is in P5 §8. This chapter is the GM's side: why the system is built this way, how to construct stat blocks quickly, and how to deploy antagonists for maximum dramatic effect.

---

## 1. The Three-Tier Model

Every human enemy in WSR belongs to one of three tiers. The tier determines mechanical complexity, narrative weight, and how much time the GM should invest in preparation.

### 1.1 Mooks

Mooks are the warm bodies that fill a dungeon, guard a gate, or crew a villain's compound. They are soldiers, bandits, city watchmen, cultist conscripts, and hired swords with no particular name. The genre demands that PCs cut through them with blood and momentum rather than grinding through each one as a full BRP combat. That is what the mook rule provides.

**Narrative role.** Mooks exist to establish threat-through-numbers, to cost the PCs a few HP before the real fight, and to be incapacitated dramatically. They are not meant to win; they are meant to make winning feel earned. A mook who lingers too long has become something else.

**Mechanical profile.** Mooks have one primary weapon skill, a Dodge rating, and an HP total. They do not have Power Points and cannot make any Fate Point spend. They do not roll on the Major Wounds Table. They fight, they absorb damage up to their threshold, and then they fall.

The mook rule: any mook who takes cumulative damage equal to or greater than half their total HP is automatically incapacitated. The GM describes how - beaten down, driven off, knocked senseless, or simply unwilling to die for six coins a day. The fiction determines the detail; the threshold determines when.

**When a mook stops being a mook.** The moment an NPC has a name in the narrative and a function in the plot, they are no longer a mook. A gate guard is a mook. A gate guard the PCs bribe twice, argue with over a contraband-laden cart, and then recognise later at the inn is no longer a mook - they are at least a professional with a name. The GM makes this call, and makes it before combat starts.

### 1.2 Professionals

Professionals are competent antagonists with individual identities. They may not be named, but they have a function: the mercenary sergeant, the cult enforcer, the treasury agent, the assassin hired for this specific job. They bring enough skill and initiative to pose a genuine threat to individual PCs, and enough characterisation to make encounters with them feel like encounters with people rather than obstacles.

**Narrative role.** Professionals are the foes who push back: they adapt to circumstances, hold their ground longer than a mook would, and often have something at stake beyond the coin. They may have opinions about the job, loyalties that complicate their instructions, or practical limits to their ruthlessness. When PCs fight professionals they should feel the difference.

**Mechanical profile.** Professionals have multiple weapon skills, a Dodge or Parry rating appropriate to their background, and a full HP total. They have limited Power Points (typically half POW or lower) but cannot make Patron Intercession rolls or Corruption trades. They have no access to the sorcery rules. They do not have Passions or Talents as chargen constructs, but the GM may assign one or two Passion-equivalent drives informally - a soldier who will die before abandoning the paymaster's vault, or a spy who panics at the first sign of sorcery.

Professionals roll on the Major Wounds Table normally when a single blow exceeds half their HP. They do not automatically incapacitate like mooks, and they do not have Fate Point access either - they simply fight until they drop.

### 1.3 Named Antagonists

Named antagonists are PC-grade characters: individuals with full stat blocks, Fate Point access, Passions, and Talents. They may have sorcery. They are the significant enemies of the campaign - the ones the players will come to dread, curse, and eventually confront in a climactic encounter. They may survive that encounter and recur.

**Narrative role.** Named antagonists drive plots. They have goals, not just instructions. They make decisions that shape events before the PCs arrive. They have something personal at stake. They represent a genuine, sustained threat that the PCs will need time, preparation, and luck to overcome.

**Mechanical profile.** Build named antagonists exactly as you would build a PC (see P2 for the chargen procedure). All the same rules apply: characteristics, derived stats, skills, talents, passions, reputation. The GM version of a named antagonist has full Fate Point access - their Power Points can fund defensive spends, and they will use them when the stakes are high enough.

For sorcerer-villain antagonists, apply the full Sorcery rules from P6. They have Corruption, may have a Patron Score, and memorise spells like any PC sorcerer. Building a sorcerer antagonist is covered in detail in G4 (NPC Sorcery and Patrons); the stat blocks in §5 of this chapter include a worked example.

## 2. Mook Stat Block Format

Mook stat blocks are designed for fast reference at the table. Keep them short. Everything the GM needs for a mook encounter fits in seven lines.

### 2.1 Mook Block Format

```
[Name/Type]

STR ##  CON ##  SIZ ##  DEX ##  POW ##  INT ##
HP ##  Dodge ##%  Damage Modifier: [none / +/-1D4 / +1D6]
Armour: [none / # AP, type]

Primary weapon: [Weapon] ##%, damage [dice]
Secondary (if any): [Weapon] ##%, damage [dice]

Incapacitated at ## HP (half of ## HP total).
No Fate Point access. No Major Wounds Table.
```

**Design notes for mook stat blocks:**

- HP should be low enough that a solid hit from any PC can threaten the threshold. HP 6-10 is the typical mook range for ancient-world soldiers and hired muscle.
- Primary weapon skill in the range 30-50% is the appropriate mook spread. At 30%, one in three attacks connects. At 50%, one in two does. Either is enough to worry PCs in numbers.
- Give mooks armour appropriate to their type, but do not over-armour them. A 3-AP leather or hide kit is realistic for most mook tiers. Heavy armour belongs on professionals.
- Do not give mooks skills beyond combat. A mook's Stealth, Spot, or Insight are irrelevant in most encounters; if they matter, you have likely promoted the NPC to a different tier.

### 2.2 The Mook Rule - GM Detail

The player-facing description of the mook rule is in P5 §8. Here is the full GM-side picture.

**Cumulative threshold.** The threshold is cumulative, not per-blow. A mook with 8 HP is incapacitated when their total damage taken reaches 4 HP - whether that happens in one solid hit or three scratches over several rounds. Track total damage, not the source of each point.

**Interaction with critical hits.** A critical attack against a mook deals full damage ignoring armour (P5 §10.4). If the full damage brings cumulative damage to or beyond half the mook's HP, they are incapacitated immediately. The GM may use the crit result for descriptive colour - how dramatically did they fall? - but the crit does not trigger Major Wounds or any other additional mechanical effect. The mook is simply done.

**Interaction with Major Wounds.** Mooks do not roll on the Major Wounds Table, ever. The mook rule replaces that system for this tier. A single blow that exceeds half the mook's HP in isolation still incapacitates them under the mook rule - it is the threshold that matters, not the source.

**Describing incapacitation.** The incapacitation is not always lethal - make the description match the fiction. A bandit struck down by a warrior's sword might be dead. The same bandit knocked sprawling by a half-spent blow and bleeding into the dust is unconscious, not necessarily dead. City guardsmen in a sovereign city do not die; they are beaten down, and the PCs now have a problem. Let the circumstances determine what "incapacitated" looks like.

**Mooks in numbers.** The reason to use the mook rule is groups: three guards, six bandits, a cult mob of eight. Track each mook's cumulative damage separately - this is not a group HP pool. But because the threshold is low, fights against groups of mooks should resolve fast. Two or three rounds should scatter most mook groups. If a mook fight is going longer than that, check whether the GM is applying the threshold correctly.

### 2.3 "Look Out Master!"

This mechanic lets a villain redirect an incoming blow to a nearby mook, spending down their Power Points to do it.

**The spend.** When a PC's attack connects with a named antagonist villain and damage is about to be resolved, the villain may spend all their remaining Power Points (minimum 8 PP) to activate this move. A mook within hand-to-hand range of the villain (or within throwing distance of a projectile attack) hurls themselves in front of the blow. The mook takes the full attack result, including any critical effects, and is immediately killed or incapacitated.

The villain takes no damage from this attack.

**Pacing function.** Look Out Master! is not a defensive tactic; it is a narrative timer. The villain has spent their entire remaining PP pool. They are now without Fate Point access, cannot buy criticals, and - if they are a sorcerer - have gutted their casting capacity. The PCs just watched their enemy burn through resources to survive. They are now vulnerable.

A GM should deploy Look Out Master! once per encounter, at the moment of maximum dramatic tension. The first time a PC gets through the villain's defences and lands a serious blow is ideal. When it triggers, name the mook who dies for it. Give them one descriptive line. Let the horror of the exchange land.

**Constraints.** The villain must have a mook within range. "Range" is defined by the attack type:

- Melee attack: one mook who is engaged or adjacent to the villain.
- Missile or thrown attack: one mook who is physically between the villain and the projectile's line.

If no mook qualifies, the mechanic cannot be used.

The PP minimum of 8 ensures this is not a free action for villains with only a couple of PP remaining. A sorcerer-villain who has been spending heavily on spells may not have 8 PP left when they need it. This is intentional.

## 3. Building Professional NPCs

Professionals are built from the same skeleton as mooks but with more flesh. The stat block is longer, the skills are broader, and the GM invests enough in the character to make them feel real in an encounter.

### 3.1 Professional Stat Block Format

```
[Name/Role]

STR ##  CON ##  SIZ ##  DEX ##  POW ##  INT ##  CHA ##
HP ##  Damage Modifier: [as applicable]
PP ##  (no Fate Point access; no sorcery)
Armour: [AP and type]

Combat skills:
  [Weapon A] ##%  damage [dice]
  [Weapon B] ##%  damage [dice]
  Dodge ##%  |  Parry (as primary weapon) ##%

Other skills: [3-5 relevant skills with %]

Notes: [any notable equipment, drives, or situational rules]
```

**Design principles:**

- **Two or three weapon skills.** A mercenary knows a sword and a backup dagger. A cult enforcer uses a staff and a short blade. Pick what fits the role, not what produces the most combinations.
- **Primary weapon skill in the 50-70% range.** This is the professional's core competency. At 60%, they connect more often than not, and their attack vs. parry math is genuinely threatening to mid-level PCs.
- **Assign a PP total but treat it as a damage buffer, not a resource pool.** A professional's PP represents resilience under stress, not access to the Fate Point menu. They will not spend it; it is there to ensure their characteristic rolls are meaningful.
- **Three to five relevant skills.** Include skills that will come up in the context where the GM introduces this NPC. A mercenary captain who commands troops needs Command and Persuade, not just sword skills. An infiltrator-type professional needs Stealth and Insight. Think about the encounter, not the archetype.
- **No Talents, no Passions as mechanics.** A professional's "drives" are notes, not mechanics. Write them as a sentence under Notes: "Will not harm children. Will flee if the paymaster is visibly dead." This is enough to run the character convincingly without chargen overhead.

### 3.2 What Professionals Are Not

Professionals are not named antagonists in waiting. Do not give them Fate Point access, Corruption tracks, or patron relationships unless you are upgrading them to a named antagonist. The middle tier should remain the middle tier. A professional who survives and becomes important enough to warrant full mechanical weight should be re-statted from scratch as a named antagonist - do not bolt extra complexity onto a professional stat block.

## 4. Building Named Antagonists

Named antagonists are PCs with different goals. Build them with the full chargen procedure from P2, adjusted for the GM's needs.

### 4.1 Named Antagonist Stat Block Format

The full stat block records everything a PC sheet would, but laid out for fast GM reference rather than player exploration.

```
[Name, role, brief descriptor]

Characteristics:
  STR ##  CON ##  SIZ ##  DEX ##  INT ##  POW ##  CHA ##

Derived:
  HP ##  PP ##  Damage Modifier: [as applicable]  MOV 10

Armour: [AP and type]

Combat skills:
  [Weapon A] ##%  damage [dice]
  [Weapon B] ##%  damage [dice]
  Dodge ##%  |  Parry ##%

Other skills: [8-12 relevant skills with %]

Talents: [name and cost; 1-2 relevant talents]
Passions: [1-3 passions with % and brief descriptor]
Reputation: ##%  [tag]

Fate Point access: yes.  PP spent on: [list options likely to be used]

[For sorcerers only:]
Corruption: ##  Breaking Point: ##
Patron Score: ## (patron name or None)
Spells in memory: [spell, level] x [number known]
Grimoire: [format]

Notes: [drives, behavioural limits, escalation notes, scenario hooks]
```

### 4.2 Designing the Antagonist's PP Budget

The villain's Power Points are the GM's primary pacing tool. A named antagonist who arrives at a final confrontation with full PP (equal to their POW) is nearly impossible to finish. One whose PP have been depleted through earlier encounters and Look Out Master! expenditures is vulnerable.

**Starting PP at encounter.** Decide at session prep how many PP the antagonist has available when the PCs finally confront them. This is a story decision, not a random determination:

- **Full PP:** The villain has been resting, prepared, and protected. This should be rare for a climactic encounter unless the PCs forced the confrontation early.
- **Half PP:** One earlier encounter, one Look Out Master! spend, or one costly spell already cast. This is the standard assumption for a mid-campaign confrontation.
- **Low PP (8 or fewer):** The villain has been grinding through the session, burning resources to survive. They are now close to the "no Look Out Master! possible" threshold. A desperate, brittle antagonist who can die in this fight.

**Tracking between encounters.** If the antagonist appears in multiple sessions, track their remaining PP and note when they recover (rest, time between sessions, patron-granted reserves). A villain who has fled one confrontation with 3 PP remaining should not arrive at the next with full reserves unless the GM deliberately grants recovery time as a story beat.

### 4.3 Sorcerer Antagonists

Sorcerer-villain antagonists have a Corruption track and may have a Patron Score. Use the full mechanics from P6 for their sorcery. Additionally:

**Spells in memory.** A sorcerer antagonist should have two or three spells actively memorised at the start of any encounter. Total spell levels memorised should not exceed half the sorcerer's INT (consistent with the PC rule in P2 §10.2). For a mid-tier sorcerer villain with INT 16, that allows up to 8 levels of spells in memory - for example, two level-3 spells and one level-2 spell.

**Casting under pressure.** Sorcerer antagonists cast the same way PC sorcerers do (see P6 §2). They share the combat interruption risk (P5 §6.5). A smart party will try to close to melee with an enemy sorcerer before they cast. A smart sorcerer antagonist will ensure they have mooks or professional bodyguards keeping melee fighters occupied.

**Corruption as a signal.** The antagonist's Corruption level tells the GM how far this sorcerer has gone. A sorcerer at low Corruption (Corruption well above their Breaking Point) is dangerous but restrained; they have not yet crossed lines they cannot uncross. A sorcerer near their Breaking Point has sold something fundamental and the GM should let that show in their behaviour, their spells, their patron relationship.

Cross-reference G4 for patron design, ritual magic, demon binding, and the deep machinery of NPC sorcery. This chapter provides the stat block; G4 provides what is behind it.

### 4.4 The Recurring Antagonist

The genre's signature villain is the one who keeps escaping. Conan's enemies don't stay dead. The sorcerer who survives the first confrontation and appears again with half-healed scars and a new plan is a campaign asset. The mechanics that make this work are simple: the villain had PP remaining when they fled, and they used it to survive (Deflect the Killing Blow or some equivalent spend). They did not escape by GM fiat; they escaped because they invested their resources into survival rather than killing.

Track surviving antagonists between sessions. Note what they have lost (PP spent, mooks killed, resources destroyed by the PCs' earlier actions) and what they have gained (rest, new allies, information about the PCs). An antagonist who has been damaged by the PCs should reflect that damage in their next appearance: physically, in their resource levels, and in how they behave.

A villain who has suffered once is cannier, warier, and probably angrier. Let that show.

## 5. Sample Stat Blocks

Five ready-to-use stat blocks, one per tier pair, calibrated for a mid-campaign WSR session. Use them directly or adjust characteristics up or down to fit your party's level of experience.

---

### 5.1 City Guard (Mook)

The gate watch, the market patrol, the temple precinct foot soldier. Not criminals; not heroes. People doing a job for coin.

```
City Guard (Mook)

STR 11  CON 11  SIZ 12  DEX 10  POW 10  INT 10
HP 12  Dodge 25%  Damage Modifier: none
Armour: 3 AP (bronze-plate reinforced leather)

Primary weapon: Spear 35%, damage 1D8+1
Backup: Short Sword 30%, damage 1D6

Incapacitated at 6 HP (half of 12 HP).
No Fate Point access. No Major Wounds Table.

Notes: Carries a whistle (1D6 rounds to summon 1D4+1 additional guards on a
success). Will typically flee or surrender at the incapacitation threshold
unless pride or watch-captain presence demands otherwise.
```

**Running city guards as mooks.** Guards work in groups. The threat is the whistle - not the spear. PCs who provoke a guard fight inside a city wall should be calculating whether they can end it before reinforcements arrive. Two guards are irritating; six are a serious problem.

City guards as individuals have no investment in dying. Their morale breaks fast. The GM should narrate incapacitation as a guard going down hard and clutching their wound, not necessarily dying - the bodies of twelve dead watchmen create different problems than twelve unconscious ones.

---

### 5.2 Bandit Raider (Mook)

The desert road-watcher, the mountain pass ambusher, the organised criminal who does not care about your documents.

```
Bandit Raider (Mook)

STR 12  CON 11  SIZ 11  DEX 12  POW 9  INT 10
HP 11  Dodge 30%  Damage Modifier: none
Armour: 1 AP (hide or cloth)

Primary weapon: Short Sword or Hand Axe 40%, damage 1D6
Secondary: Dagger 35%, damage 1D4+2

Incapacitated at 6 HP (rounded from half of 11 HP).
No Fate Point access. No Major Wounds Table.

Notes: Bandits fight for profit. Once the fight looks costly, they reassess.
If two or more are incapacitated in the same round, remaining bandits may
attempt to disengage (the GM may call for a Morale read: the first one who
runs takes the rest with them).
```

**Running bandit raiders.** Bandits are opportunists. They will not fight to the last unless their leader - a professional or named antagonist - is holding them in the fight with presence and fear. A bandit pack without strong leadership should buckle as soon as the math turns against them.

The low armour makes bandits squishier than city guards. Their slightly higher weapon skills make them more threatening attack-side. An ambush from cover where the PCs have no time to prepare is when bandits are most dangerous.

---

### 5.3 Mercenary Captain (Professional)

A veteran soldier-for-hire with a decade of campaigns behind them. Knows more about keeping their people alive than about winning wars. Will follow the paymaster's orders until the paymaster stops making sense.

```
Mercenary Captain

STR 14  CON 14  SIZ 13  DEX 13  POW 11  INT 12  CHA 13
HP 14  Damage Modifier: +1D4  MOV 10
PP 11  (no Fate Point access; no sorcery)
Armour: 5 AP (scale and bronze helmet)

Combat skills:
  Sword 65%  damage 1D8+1+1D4
  Shield (Large Round) 55%  damage 1D6+1D4  |  Parry 55%
  Javelin 50%  damage 1D6+1D4  (thrown, 3 javelins carried)
  Dodge 35%

Other skills:
  Command 60%
  Insight 55%
  Persuade 45%
  Strategy 50%
  First Aid 40%

Notes: Commands up to a dozen mercenaries (use Bandit Raider or City Guard
as mook stat blocks for the troops). Will not abandon wounded subordinates
unless specifically ordered to. Has a grudge against sorcerers (bad
campaign experience - the GM can make this a useful lever or a
complication). Equipment includes a silver arm-ring worth 4 Staters.
```

**Running the mercenary captain.** This character fights smart. They will use their Command skill to direct mooks, use their strategy awareness to take the best tactical ground, and keep a shield in front of them for as long as possible. They are not a berserker; they are a professional who has survived by not taking unnecessary risks.

At 60% sword and 55% shield, the captain's attack-parry combination is a genuine threat to any PC who does not have comparable weapon skills. A PC warrior expecting an easy fight will be disappointed.

The captain will flee if the paymaster is visibly dead and the remaining pay is not worth dying for. They might also switch sides - for a price.

### 5.4 Cult Priest (Professional)

The temple enforcer, the inquisitor of the hidden faith, the keeper of the inner mysteries who also knows how to hurt people. This NPC represents the human arm of a dangerous cult - not a sorcerer, but someone who has seen enough sorcery that they are not frightened of it.

```
Cult Priest

STR 11  CON 12  SIZ 12  DEX 11  POW 14  INT 14  CHA 15
HP 12  Damage Modifier: none  MOV 10
PP 14  (no Fate Point access; no sorcery)
Armour: 3 AP (lamellar coat under ceremonial robes)

Combat skills:
  Morningstar 50%  damage 1D8
  Dagger 45%  damage 1D4+2
  Dodge 40%

Other skills:
  Insight 70%
  Intimidate 65%
  Persuade 60%
  Knowledge (Religion) 75%
  Knowledge (Occult) 55%
  Research 50%
  Status 60%

Notes: Commands 2D6 cultist followers (treat as mooks: HP 8, primary weapon
30%, Dodge 20%, incapacitated at 4 HP, no armour). Uses Intimidate and
Persuade before weapons; has long experience reading interrogation situations
and exploiting emotional pressure points. Will not fight the PCs directly if
the cult's true sorcerer is present - defers to power. If the true sorcerer
is eliminated, may attempt to bargain with the PCs (knows things; is willing
to trade information for survival).
```

**Running the cult priest.** The priest's combat skills are secondary to their social and informational skills. They are most dangerous when they know something the PCs need and are willing to use that knowledge as leverage. In a fight, they fight defensively and try to protect the sorcerer or the ritual they are conducting.

The high Insight (70%) means this character reads the PCs quickly. They know who is afraid, who is bluffing, and who is genuinely going to do violence. The GM should play them as acutely observant and carefully calculating, not as a zealot who charges with a mace.

The cultist followers as mooks have deliberately weak stats - they are true believers with improvised weapons, not trained soldiers. They will die for the faith if commanded, but the command must come from the priest or the cult's sorcerer.

### 5.5 Tribal Champion (Named Antagonist)

The great fighter of a warrior culture: elected by blood-right, feared by reputation, and absolutely certain of their own excellence. The kind of opponent a PC warrior might duel in the third act of an adventure.

```
Dhakar the Unbeaten, Champion of the Red Sands Tribe

STR 17  CON 16  SIZ 15  DEX 16  INT 12  POW 12  CHA 13
HP 16  PP 12  Damage Modifier: +1D6  MOV 10
Armour: 3 AP (hide and bone-plate reinforcement)

Combat skills:
  Spear 75%  damage 1D8+2+1D6
  Thrown Spear 65%  damage 1D8+2+1D6
  Hand Axe 60%  damage 1D6+2+1D6
  Brawl 55%  damage 1D3+1D6
  Dodge 50%

Other skills:
  Command 55%
  Intimidate 65%
  Survival 60%
  Track 55%
  Navigate 45%

Talents:
  Bone-Breaker (Combat, 4 PP): On a successful melee attack, add +1D6 damage.
  Unbreakable (Combat, 3 PP): Auto-succeed one Stamina roll to avoid
    unconsciousness from a wound (once per combat).

Passions:
  Honour of the bloodline 80% - will not attack from ambush or strike an
    unarmed opponent who has yielded.
  Hatred of sorcery 70% - will refuse to work with or accept aid from
    sorcerers; the GM may invoke this to complicate alliances.
  Loyalty to the tribe 60%.

Reputation: 35%  ("Dhakar the Unbeaten - forty duels, no defeat")

Fate Point access: yes.  Likely spends: Bone-Breaker (damage burst), Deflect
the Killing Blow (if clearly losing), Stand Your Ground (prevent
incapacitation while still fighting).

Notes: Dhakar enters a duel formally - challenge is expected, terms are set,
witnesses count. Fighting him in an ambush costs social credibility with the
tribe and triggers his Hatred of sorcery passion if any magic is involved.
He will not flee from a duel unless the opponent reveals sorcerous power, at
which point he considers the duel forfeit and will withdraw with contempt. He
collects the weapons of opponents he has defeated; currently carries six
besides his own. A skilled persuader can learn what debts his tribe owes to
the powers that sent them raiding.
```

**Running Dhakar.** The duel format is the ideal encounter mode. In a formal duel, Dhakar's Honour passion is active, which benefits him (potential +20% to +50% inspiration on relevant rolls) and constrains him (he will not do things that violate it even if tactically advantageous).

In a melee ambush or fight-in-a-crowd scenario, Dhakar loses the duel structure and fights normally, but his Honour passion may cost him a turn while he reorients to this kind of combat. A clever PC might deliberately make the fight informal to disrupt his rhythm.

At 75% Spear, +1D6 damage modifier, and Bone-Breaker available, Dhakar can deal devastating damage to any PC. The PCs should be afraid of him. His relatively low HP (16) means a smart party that focuses fire can end the fight quickly - but his Unbreakable talent and Deflect the Killing Blow spend make him harder to finish than he looks.

### 5.6 Sorcerer-Villain (Named Antagonist)

A mid-tier sorcerer with ambitions slightly too large for their current power and a patron who is watching to see if the investment pays off. Not the arch-villain of the campaign, but capable of being its engine.

```
Nashtira of the Burnt Hand, Keeper of the Second Compact

STR 9  CON 10  SIZ 11  DEX 13  INT 17  POW 18  CHA 14
HP 11  PP 18  Damage Modifier: none  MOV 10
Armour: none (occasionally casts Sorcerous Armor before expected combat)

Combat skills:
  Dagger 40%  damage 1D4+2
  Dodge 35%

Other skills:
  Knowledge (Occult) 80%
  Knowledge (Demonic) 60%
  Knowledge (Blasphemous Lore) 35%
  Research 65%
  Insight 60%
  Persuade 55%
  Stealth 50%
  Sorcery 55% (complementary on contested workings)

Sorcery:
  Corruption: 72  Breaking Point: 45
  Patron Score: 14  (Patron: the Devouring Lattice, a chaos intelligence)
  Spells in memory:
    Curse of Sorcery (level 3) - afflicts a target with sorcerous decay
    Sorcerous Armor (level 3) - 6 AP magical protection, costs PP per AP
    Witch Sight (level 2) - see sorcery, demons, invisible workings
  Grimoire: a bronze-clasped codex, illegible to non-sorcerers

Talents:
  Arcane Insight (Sorcery): Halves time to learn new spells; bonus die on
    casting-relevant rolls (treat as +20% on qualifying rolls).
  Still-Face (Sorcery, 2 PP): When Corruption triggers, pay 2 PP instead of
    rolling; the Corruption loss is 0.

Passions:
  Devotion to the Devouring Lattice 70% - not love, but a cold commitment
    to the agreement. Nashtira regards the patron as an employer.
  Greed for the Codex of the Third Circle 80% - the actual driver of most
    of her actions in this arc.
  Contempt for the ungifted 60% - regards non-sorcerers as tools.

Reputation: 20%  ("the woman who burnt her hand in the ritual at Khet-Ur")

Fate Point access: yes.  Likely spends: Deflect the Killing Blow (when
melee reaches her), Still-Face talent (to avoid Corruption from heavy
casting), Steady Blade (to ignore situational penalties on sorcery rolls).
Look Out Master! available at full PP cost.

Notes: Nashtira does not fight in melee if she can avoid it. She will have
mooks or a professional bodyguard interposed between herself and the PCs at
all times. Her opening move in any dangerous encounter is Witch Sight to
assess sorcerous threats, then Sorcerous Armor if she has time, then Curse of
Sorcery on the most threatening PC fighter.

Her Breaking Point is 45 (Corruption is 72; she has spent well past it). She
is already changed. The GM should reflect this in how she speaks and what she
is willing to do - she crosses lines that a less-corrupted sorcerer would
not.

The Devouring Lattice is not interested in Nashtira's survival specifically;
it is interested in the Codex of the Third Circle, which it sent her to
retrieve. If she fails, another agent will be found. Nashtira knows this,
which explains both her ruthlessness and her desperation.

Cross-reference G4 for the Devouring Lattice's full patron profile and the
Patron Intercession mechanics at Patron Score 14.
```

**Running Nashtira.** She is physically fragile (HP 11, no armour by default) but magically dangerous (POW 18 means her Resistance Table rolls against PC POW are favourable). The PCs should be able to kill her quickly if they reach melee - the challenge is getting through the mooks and bodyguard.

Her Corruption past her Breaking Point is a roleplaying and GM-description tool. She should seem wrong: too still, too calm, capable of suggestions or decisions that a normal person would flinch from. The patron connection is the long-game pressure - Nashtira knows the patron will not save her unless the Codex is in play, so she will spend her own resources before calling on Patron Intercession.

Look Out Master! is available if she has full PP at the moment the PCs break through. After a session in which she has cast multiple spells, it may not be - she may be reduced to running rather than spending, which is a dramatically satisfying outcome and an invitation for a chase scene.

## 6. Encounter Design Notes

A few principles for deploying the three-tier model in play.

**Mix tiers deliberately.** The most interesting encounters involve mooks, one professional, and the shadow of a named antagonist who is not yet present. The professional gives the mooks direction and makes the encounter feel like more than a numbers problem. The offstage named antagonist gives the whole encounter stakes beyond this particular fight.

**Named antagonists do not fight every session.** A recurring named antagonist who the PCs fight in every session will be ground down in three sessions. Place them in encounters at moments of maximum dramatic consequence: once to establish their threat, once to raise the stakes, and once for the confrontation that resolves or escalates the arc. In between, they operate through intermediaries - professionals and mooks carrying out instructions.

**The PP economy is the encounter economy.** Every mook that uses Look Out Master! on behalf of a villain, every professional who makes the PCs spend a Fate Point, every hard fight that costs the party PP is preparation for the final encounter. GMs who care about dramatic pacing track not just what the PCs have spent but what the antagonist has spent, and they tune the final confrontation's PP starting level accordingly.

**Calibrate mook HP to the party's damage output.** If the PCs are rolling with weapons that deal 1D8+1D6 on average, a mook with HP 6 will go down in one hit - which is dramatically satisfying but may feel too easy. A mook with HP 10 will usually survive one hit but not two. Calibrate to produce tension without drag: two rounds to scatter a mook group is ideal; four rounds is too long.

**Give professionals a reason to be there.** A professional who is just "guard number four with better stats" is a missed opportunity. The mercenary captain is there because someone is paying them. The cult enforcer is there because the priest sent them. The spy is there because someone tipped off the guild. A sentence of context makes a professional feel like a person in a world rather than an encounter difficulty slider.
